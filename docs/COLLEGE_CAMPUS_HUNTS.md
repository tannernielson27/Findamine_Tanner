# College campus hunts: draft for review

Four draft hunts written for the BYU undergraduate cohort (audience: adults, grades 13-16).
They were seeded into the findamine-tanner database by `supabase/seed/college_campus_hunts.sql`. Make changes in the app's hunt editor: re-running the SQL replaces the drafts and discards edits made in the app.

## Before publishing

1. **Place every pin.** Coordinates are approximate, copied from older seed data. Each stop is flagged *needs placement*, and publishing is blocked until you open each stop in the editor, drag the pin to the real spot and save.
2. **Check the facts marked ⚠.** I wrote them from general knowledge; confirm them on site or against BYU's own history pages.
3. **Check the small claims in clues**, like free entry at the Bean Museum and the Museum of Art, and building directions such as "north side" or "south of the library".
4. **Walk each hunt once on a phone.**

Open-ended stops (no answer key) count any honest submission as complete. Keyed stops use exact matching, which is why answers with a key are multiple choice or ordering.

## The Founders' File

*An archivist started reconstructing how a one-room academy became a 30,000-student university, then left the file half-finished. Five stops, one timeline, and a few things you can only check in person.*

Subjects: history_community, critical_thinking · ~40 min · difficulty: ascending

> You've inherited a folder of loose notes and no dates in order. Each stop recovers a piece. By the library, you should be able to put the whole thing back together.

⚠ Verify: founded 1875 · Maeser principal from 1876 · renamed BYU in 1903 · Y built 1906, ~380 ft · Maeser Building finished 1911 and the first on upper campus · Smoot was a Provo mayor and academy backer · the chalk-circle quote and 'better self' attribution.

### 1. Brigham Square

**Clue:** Start where campus does its people-watching: the open square at the heart of upper campus, named for the man who signed the academy's founding deed.

**Clue hints:** Upper campus, central, wide-open plaza. → It sits between the library and the oldest buildings on the hill. → Look for the plaza called Brigham Square.

**Primer, An academy, not a university:** Brigham Young Academy was founded in Provo in 1875. Karl G. Maeser took charge as principal in 1876 and shaped its culture for years. The school operated as an academy, teaching everything from primary grades up, until it was renamed Brigham Young University in 1903.

**Task (multiple choice), Academy years:** How many years did the school run as an academy before it became a university?

Options: 18 / **28** ✓ / 32 / 46

### 2. Karl G. Maeser Building

**Clue:** Find the oldest building on the hill: stone columns, a principal's name, and the closest thing upper campus has to a cornerstone.

**Clue hints:** It faces the square you just left. → Look for classical columns on a very old stone facade. → It's the Karl G. Maeser Building.

**Primer, The chalk circle:** Maeser is remembered for a line about honor: lock him behind stone walls and he might find a way out, but draw a chalk circle on the floor and have him promise not to cross it, and he never would. He also told students to 'be yourself, but always your better self.' The building named for him was finished in 1911, the first on this hill.

**Task (short text), Walls or words:** Maeser bet on a promise over stone walls. Standing in front of a building made to last a century, write one or two sentences: what actually lasts longer at a university, its buildings or its reputation? Use one detail you can see from here.

Open-ended: any real submission completes it.

### 3. Abraham O. Smoot Administration Building

**Clue:** Money keeps a school alive. The building that runs this place carries the name of the businessman who kept the academy afloat in its leanest years.

**Clue hints:** It's the administration building, close to the Maeser Building. → Students just call it by three letters. → The ASB: Abraham O. Smoot Building.

**Primer, Who pays the bills:** Abraham O. Smoot, a Provo businessman and former mayor, was one of the academy's main financial backers and served on its board. Universities often name buildings after donors, founders and leaders, which makes a campus map a record of who mattered to the institution, and when.

**Task (photo observation), Read the evidence:** Find a date carved, cast or posted anywhere on a building you can see from here: a cornerstone, plaque or dedication. Photograph it (no people in the frame).

Open-ended: any real submission completes it.

### 4. East-facing view of Y Mountain

**Clue:** Look up and east. The biggest letter in Utah County has been on that mountainside since students carried it up there on their backs.

**Clue hints:** Head to the east edge of upper campus. → Any spot with a clear line of sight to the mountain works. → You're looking for the Y on Y Mountain.

**Primer, The letter on the hill:** Students built the Y on the mountain east of campus in 1906, hauling materials up by hand. It's roughly 380 feet tall, far bigger than it looks from here, and it has been rebuilt and whitewashed many times since.

**Task (multiple choice), Count back:** The Y went up in 1906. How many years after the academy's founding was that?

Options: 21 / 28 / **31** ✓ / 41

### 5. Harold B. Lee Library

**Clue:** Close the file where everyone on campus eventually ends up at 11 p.m.: the library at the center of upper campus.

**Clue hints:** It's the biggest building near Brigham Square. → Most of it is underground. → The HBLL.

**Task (sorting ordering), Close the file:** Put the archivist's notes back in order, earliest first.

Correct order: Brigham Young Academy is founded → Karl G. Maeser takes charge as principal → The academy is renamed Brigham Young University → Students build the Y on the mountain → The Maeser Building is finished

## Field Notes: Campus Ecology

*Campus is a working ecosystem hiding in plain sight. Read a pond, track some birds, take a surface temperature, and connect it all to the ancient lake this whole valley used to be.*

Subjects: science_nature, geography_maps · ~45 min · difficulty: mixed

> You're filing field notes for a survey of campus ecology. Each stop is one observation plus one idea that explains it.

⚠ Verify: the pin names and locations for Botany Pond, the duck pond, the Benson garden and the Bean Museum · the carp/June sucker summary (consistent with the June Sucker Recovery Program) · the Lake Bonneville dates.

### 1. Botany Pond

**Clue:** A small pond with a big name: tucked by the life-sciences buildings and planted to be studied, not just admired.

**Clue hints:** West side of upper campus, near the science buildings. → Look for water with deliberate plantings around it. → Botany Pond.

**Primer, Reading a pond:** Ponds turn green when algae explode, usually because extra nitrogen and phosphorus wash in from fertilized lawns, gardens and storm drains. This is eutrophication. When the algae die and decompose, bacteria use up the dissolved oxygen, which can stress or kill fish.

**Task (multiple choice), Pea-soup pond:** A campus pond turns pea-soup green every August. Which is the most likely driver?

Options: **Nutrient runoff from fertilized lawns** ✓ / Too many fish in the pond / Water that's too cold / Not enough sunlight

### 2. Duck Pond

**Clue:** Follow the quacking. Campus has a second, larger pond where the wildlife has clearly figured out that students carry snacks.

**Clue hints:** North-west of upper campus. → Bigger than Botany Pond, with lots of resident birds. → The duck pond.

**Primer, Counting like a biologist:** Field biologists use timed counts to compare sites: watch for a fixed time, count distinct species (not individuals), and note behavior. Repeated over seasons, simple counts reveal migration, nesting and the effect of human feeding.

**Task (numeric entry), Three-minute survey:** Set a 3-minute timer. How many distinct bird species can you identify here? (Can't name them? Count clearly different kinds.)

Open-ended: any real submission completes it.

### 3. Benson Building garden

**Clue:** Head for the building where plant and animal sciences live. The landscaping outside is doing more science than it lets on.

**Clue hints:** It's the life-sciences building on the south-west side of campus. → Look for planted beds outside the entrance. → The Benson Building.

**Primer, Why concrete runs hot:** Dark, hard surfaces like asphalt absorb most sunlight (low albedo) and release it as heat; plants cool their surroundings by shading and by evaporating water. That combination is why cities, and big parking lots, run several degrees hotter than parks: the urban heat island effect.

**Task (short text), Surface check:** Within 20 meters of you, find the hottest and coolest surfaces you can (carefully use the back of your hand). Name both and explain the difference using albedo, shade or evaporation.

Open-ended: any real submission completes it.

### 4. Monte L. Bean Life Science Museum

**Clue:** Find the building full of animals that hold very still. Free entry, lots of taxidermy, and the best case for Utah biodiversity on campus.

**Clue hints:** It's a museum, not a classroom building. → Look for the natural-history museum on the north side of campus. → The Bean Life Science Museum.

**Primer, Utah Lake's unwanted guest:** Common carp were introduced to Utah Lake over a century ago and now make up most of its fish biomass. Carp feed by rooting through the lakebed, uprooting plants and clouding the water. The native June sucker, found nowhere else, crashed toward extinction as its habitat degraded, and large-scale carp removal is part of its recovery plan.

**Task (multiple choice), Invasion mechanics:** How did introduced carp hurt Utah Lake's native June sucker the most?

Options: **By destroying habitat: uprooting plants and muddying the water** ✓ / By eating adult June suckers / By raising the lake's temperature / By making the water saltier

### 5. West-facing view of Utah Lake

**Clue:** End with the long view: find a spot on the west side of campus where you can see the lake and the valley floor below.

**Clue hints:** Head to the west edge of campus. → You want a clear view toward the water. → Utah Lake is the big, flat water to the west.

**Primer, You're standing on a beach:** About 15,000 to 30,000 years ago, Lake Bonneville covered much of western Utah. As it drained and dried, it left shoreline terraces (benches) along the mountains, including one named after Provo. Utah Lake and the Great Salt Lake are what's left of it.

**Task (multiple choice), Ancient lake:** Utah Lake is a remnant of which Ice Age lake?

Options: **Lake Bonneville** ✓ / Lake Lahontan / Lake Missoula / Lake Agassiz

## Campus by the Numbers

*Pace it, estimate it, calculate it. A Fermi-estimation tour of campus for people who'd rather reason to an answer than look it up.*

Subjects: math_real_world, critical_thinking · ~40 min · difficulty: ascending

> Every stop is a question you could Google, but you'll get closer, faster, by estimating. Bring your stride and a phone calculator.

Math answers come from numbers stated in the question, so they hold even if a real-world figure is rough. ⚠ The Y trail figures (~1,070 ft over ~1.2 mi) are commonly quoted; confirm them if you mention them out loud.

### 1. Brigham Square

**Clue:** Start in the biggest open plaza on upper campus. You'll be measuring it with your feet.

**Clue hints:** Central upper campus. → Between the library and the oldest buildings. → Brigham Square.

**Primer, Fermi estimation:** Fermi estimates break a hard question into rough pieces you can measure or guess, then multiply. An average walking stride is about 0.75 m. A packed concert crowd is about 4 people per square meter; a comfortable crowd is closer to 1.

**Task (numeric entry), Crowd capacity:** Pace off the plaza's length and width. At concert density (about 4 people per m²), roughly how many people could fit here?

Open-ended: any real submission completes it.

### 2. Harold B. Lee Library

**Clue:** Next: the building where most of the campus's square footage is hiding underground.

**Clue hints:** Right next to the square. → Most of it is below ground level. → The HBLL.

**Task (multiple choice), Shelf math:** A typical library shelf holds about 30 books per meter. A library with 100 km of shelving holds roughly how many books?

Options: 300,000 / **3 million** ✓ / 30 million / 300 million

### 3. East-facing view of Y Mountain

**Clue:** Find a clear line of sight to the giant letter on the mountain. You're about to find out how steep the hike to it is.

**Clue hints:** East edge of upper campus. → Look up and east. → The Y on Y Mountain.

**Primer, Grade, not angle:** Trail steepness is usually given as grade: vertical rise divided by horizontal distance, as a percentage. A 10% grade rises 10 feet for every 100 feet traveled. One mile is 5,280 feet.

**Task (multiple choice), How steep is the Y?:** The Y trail climbs about 1,070 feet over roughly 1.2 miles. What's its average grade?

Options: About 9% / **About 17%** ✓ / About 34% / About 89%

### 4. Eyring Science Center

**Clue:** Head to the physics building. You'll be measuring something tall without climbing it.

**Clue hints:** East side of upper campus. → It's where physics and astronomy live. → The Eyring Science Center.

**Primer, Similar triangles:** At any moment, the sun hits everything nearby at the same angle, so the ratio of height to shadow is the same for you and for a lamppost. If you know your height and measure both shadows, the lamppost's height = your height × (its shadow ÷ your shadow).

**Task (numeric entry), Shadow measurement:** Pick a lamppost, sign or tree near this building. Pace off its shadow and your own. Using your height, estimate its height in meters. (Overcast? Estimate by stacking your own height instead.)

Open-ended: any real submission completes it.

### 5. Joseph F. Smith Building

**Clue:** Finish at the big glass-and-brick home of the humanities and family sciences, south of the library.

**Clue hints:** South of the HBLL. → Large atrium, lots of glass. → The JFSB.

**Task (multiple choice), Late to class:** Walking speed is about 5 km/h. If your next class is 1.5 km away, about how many minutes do you need?

Options: 8 minutes / **18 minutes** ✓ / 30 minutes / 45 minutes

## Read the Room: Art & Argument

*A walking seminar in noticing, persuading and spotting bad arguments, using campus art and architecture as the source material.*

Subjects: reading_writing, critical_thinking · ~40 min · difficulty: mixed

> You're writing a short critic's notebook: a few lines on what you see, and a sharper eye for how people try to convince you of things.

⚠ Verify: the Museum of Art pin, and that there's an outdoor sculpture with a readable plaque near the stop-2 pin (or move it to one).

### 1. Museum of Art entrance

**Clue:** Start at campus's dedicated art museum: big, modern, free to enter, and usually hosting at least one exhibit people argue about.

**Clue hints:** It's a standalone museum building, not inside a classroom building. → Look for the large modern building with a broad front plaza. → The Museum of Art (MOA).

**Primer, Constraint makes it sharper:** Six-word stories, like the one often attributed to Hemingway ('For sale: baby shoes, never worn'), work by implying a bigger story than they tell. Constraints force you to choose the single detail that does the most work.

**Task (creative writing), Six words:** Look around the entrance and plaza. Write a six-word story inspired by something you can see right now.

Open-ended: any real submission completes it.

### 2. Outdoor sculpture on campus

**Clue:** Campus has public sculpture scattered across it. Find any one piece, preferably one you've walked past a hundred times without reading the plaque.

**Clue hints:** Check plazas, building entrances and garden beds. → Look for a plaque at the base. → Any outdoor sculpture with a plaque works.

**Task (short text), Read the plaque:** Write the sculpture's title and artist from the plaque, then one sentence on what you think the artist wanted a passerby to feel.

Open-ended: any real submission completes it.

### 3. Harold B. Lee Library

**Clue:** Head to the building with the most posters per square meter on campus. Study the bulletin boards like a rhetorician.

**Clue hints:** Central upper campus. → Big, mostly underground, very busy. → The HBLL.

**Primer, Classic persuasion moves:** Bandwagon appeals say everyone's doing it. Appeals to authority lean on who's saying it rather than the evidence. Scarcity says act now before it's gone. None of these is automatically wrong, but each is a shortcut that can replace an actual reason.

**Task (multiple choice), Name the move:** A flyer reads: 'Everyone's switching to the new study rooms. Don't get left behind!' Which technique is it leaning on?

Options: **Bandwagon** ✓ / Appeal to authority / Scarcity / Straw man

### 4. Joseph F. Smith Building

**Clue:** Find the home of the humanities: the building where English, philosophy and history majors spend most of their week.

**Clue hints:** South of the library. → Large central atrium. → The JFSB.

**Primer, Attack the argument, not the person:** An ad hominem fallacy dismisses a claim because of who made it rather than whether it's true. A straw man misrepresents an opponent's view to make it easier to knock down. Both are common in comment sections and campus debates alike.

**Task (multiple choice), Spot the fallacy:** Which of these is an ad hominem?

Options: **'You can't trust her budget plan; she failed accounting once.'** ✓ / 'If we extend library hours, students will have more time to study.' / 'Most surveyed students prefer later classes, so we should test it.' / 'This policy costs more, but it saves time.'

### 5. Brigham Square

**Clue:** End at the central plaza named after a founder, a fitting spot for the last question.

**Clue hints:** Central upper campus. → Wide-open plaza between the library and the Maeser Building. → Brigham Square.

**Task (short text), Take a side:** Universities across the country have debated renaming buildings. In two sentences: should a campus ever change a building's name? Give one reason, and name the strongest objection to your own view.

Open-ended: any real submission completes it.
